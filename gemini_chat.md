There is no single "most important" indicator in options trading because options depend on both stock price action and unique pricing mechanics. Instead, experienced options traders rely on a combination of Implied Volatility (IV) for pricing/cost, Delta (or Open Interest) for probability and positioning, and a trend/momentum indicator (like Moving Averages or RSI) for direction. [1] (https://tradetron.tech/blog/best-indicators-for-options-trading), [2] (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jZfbx1LbOwo), [3] (https://www.youtube.com/shorts/Zh5ifSmPKgA)

1. The Most Crucial Option-Specific Indicator: Implied Volatility (IV)

• What it does: Measures the market's forecast of a move in the underlying asset and dictates how expensive options contracts are. [1] (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jZfbx1LbOwo)
• Why it matters: Unlike stock trading, you can be right about the direction of a stock and still lose money on an option if IV crushes (collapses) after a major event like an earnings announcement. [1] (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jZfbx1LbOwo)
• Key rule: High IV makes options expensive (favorable for selling premiums); low IV makes options cheap (favorable for buying premiums). You can analyze volatility trends using tools like Barchart Insights. [1] (https://www.barchart.com/story/news/516652/the-only-4-technical-indicators-options-traders-actually-need), [2] (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jZfbx1LbOwo)

2. The Key Risk & Probability Indicator: The Greeks (Specifically Delta)

• What it does: Measures sensitivity of the option's price to various factors. [1] (https://www.youtube.com/shorts/Zh5ifSmPKgA)
• Delta: Acts as a rough estimate of the probability that an option will expire in-the-money (e.g., a Delta of 0.50 means a roughly 50% chance or a $0.50 change in option price for a $1.00 move in the stock). Long options traders often target a Delta between 0.50 and 0.60. [1] (https://www.reddit.com/r/Daytrading/comments/1cw6dms/what_is_your_favorite_indicator_or_study_you_use/), [2] (https://www.youtube.com/shorts/Zh5ifSmPKgA)
• Theta: Measures time decay—how much value the option loses every single day, which is vital because options have an expiration date. [1] (https://www.investopedia.com/articles/active-trading/101314/top-technical-indicators-options-trading.asp), [2] (https://www.youtube.com/shorts/Zh5ifSmPKgA)

3. The Directional & Timing Indicators (Price Action)

Because options are time-sensitive, you need a way to gauge trend direction and momentum so you don't burn through your time before the move happens: [1] (https://www.barchart.com/story/news/516652/the-only-4-technical-indicators-options-traders-actually-need), [2] (https://www.investopedia.com/articles/active-trading/101314/top-technical-indicators-options-trading.asp)
• Moving Averages (SMA/EMA): Used to identify whether you are trading with or against the core trend of the underlying stock (e.g., 20-day, 50-day, or 200-day averages). [1] (https://tradetron.tech/blog/best-indicators-for-options-trading), [2] (https://www.barchart.com/story/news/516652/the-only-4-technical-indicators-options-traders-actually-need)
• Relative Strength Index (RSI): A momentum oscillator (0 to 100) that helps spot overbought (>70) or oversold (<30) conditions to time your entries. [1] (https://www.investopedia.com/articles/active-trading/101314/top-technical-indicators-options-trading.asp)
• Bollinger Bands: Measures volatility expansion and contraction. A narrow band "squeeze" often precedes a sharp breakout. [1] (https://www.investopedia.com/articles/active-trading/101314/top-technical-indicators-options-trading.asp), [2] (https://www.barchart.com/story/news/516652/the-only-4-technical-indicators-options-traders-actually-need)

For selling options on a swing trading horizon (holding for weeks), Implied Volatility Percentile/Rank (IVP/IVR) is your most important indicator [1].
When you sell options, your primary goal is to collect premium and benefit from time decay (Theta) and falling volatility (Vega) [1].

The Ultimate Swing Selling Indicator: IV Rank / IV Percentile

• What it does: Tells you whether the current Implied Volatility is high or low compared to that specific stock's historical average.
• Why it matters: Standard IV only tells you the current number, but IV Rank tells you if it's cheap or expensive. You want to sell when IV Rank is above 50%.
• The Math: If a stock has an IV of 40%, it might look low. But if its historical IV range is 10% to 45%, an IV of 40% is actually extremely high (an IV Rank of ~85%).
• The Strategy: High IV Rank means option premiums are heavily inflated. You sell the expensive premium, and profit as the stock stabilizes and IV reverts to its mean (called an "IV Crush").

The Operational Indicators Framework

To execute a 30-to-45-day option selling swing trade (such as a Credit Spread or Cash-Secured Put), use this multi-layered framework:

Indicator Category	Specific Indicator	Optimal Metric / Target	Actionable Purpose
Volatility (Price/Cost)	IV Rank (IVR)	> 50%	Ensures you are getting paid maximum premium for the risk.
Probability (Strike Selection)	Delta (Δ)	0.15 to 0.30	Gives you a statistically high probability of success (~70% to 85% chance of expiring worthless).
Time Decay (Duration)	Days to Expiration (DTE)	30 to 45 Days	Captures the acceleration of the Theta decay curve before it drops off too sharply.
Directional Filter	50-day / 200-day EMA	Stock above EMAs for bullish sells; below for bearish sells.	Avoids selling premium directly into a massive, runaway institutional trend.


Managing the Trade: The Theta Decay Curve

When swing trading short options, you do not want to hold until expiration day. The sweet spot for entering a trade is 30 to 45 days to expiration. This is where the time decay curve begins to steepen significantly, allowing you to buy back the option early for a profit.

The Golden Rules for Options Sellers

1. Sell High IV, Buy Low IV: Use stock screeners to look specifically for tickers with elevated IV Percentiles, often found right before earnings or during market pullbacks.
2. Manage Winners Early: Do not try to squeeze out the last 5% to 10% of profit. Buy back your short options when you hit 50% of maximum profit to completely eliminate tail risk and free up capital for the next swing.

Implied Volatility (IV) is not calculated directly; it is "back-solved" from the actual market price of an option. [1] (https://www.investopedia.com/ask/answers/032515/what-options-implied-volatility-and-how-it-calculated.asp), [2] (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HEKs61EK-5Y&vl=en)
Unlike metrics like historical volatility, which look backward at previous stock price charts, IV is forward-looking and reflects what the market thinks the stock will do next. It is determined using a mathematical process called reverse engineering an option pricing model (most commonly the Black-Scholes-Merton Model). [1] (https://questdb.com/glossary/implied-volatility-calculation/), [2] (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=aYpLyeWu7Ac&t=572), [3] (https://www.investopedia.com/ask/answers/032515/what-options-implied-volatility-and-how-it-calculated.asp), [4] (https://www.schwab.com/learn/story/aligning-your-options-with-implied-volatility)

The Forward-Solving Formula (Standard Pricing)

In a standard options equation, you input the known factual data to find the "fair value" or theoretical price of an option: [1] (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HEKs61EK-5Y&vl=en)
• Current Stock Price
• Strike Price
• Days to Expiration
• Risk-Free Interest Rate
• Dividends (if applicable)
• Expected Volatility (The unknown variable) [1] (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2OaOxtk1ky8&t=36), [2] (https://www.investopedia.com/ask/answers/032515/what-options-implied-volatility-and-how-it-calculated.asp), [3] (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=aYpLyeWu7Ac&t=572)
\(\text{Inputs\ (including\ Volatility)}\longrightarrow \text{Pricing\ Model}\longrightarrow \text{Theoretical\ Option\ Price}\)

The Backward-Solving Formula (Finding IV)

In the real world, the open market determines the actual option price via live supply and demand (bids and asks). Because computers already know the live price and all the other variables, the formula is flipped on its head: [1] (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Implied_volatility), [2] (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=aYpLyeWu7Ac&t=572), [3] (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=h6fMYoig2Pg&t=1)
\(\text{Factual\ Inputs\ +\ Live\ Market\ Price}\longrightarrow \text{Pricing\ Model\ Inversion}\longrightarrow \text{Implied\ Volatility\ (IV)}\)
Because the Black-Scholes algebraic formula cannot be cleanly isolated to solve for volatility directly, software programs use numerical iteration (trial and error). The system plugs in different percentages until it finds the exact volatility level that forces the formula's output to match the real-time market price. [1] (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UQFuf5EfyK0), [2] (https://www.investopedia.com/ask/answers/032515/what-options-implied-volatility-and-how-it-calculated.asp), [3] (https://people.stat.ucla.edu/nchristo/statistics_c183_c283/statc183c283_implied_volatility.pdf)

The Human Element: What Actually Drives IV Up or Down?

While the math handles the calculation, buyer and seller behavior is what actually forces IV to change. [1] (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=aYpLyeWu7Ac&t=572)
• The "Fear Gauge" Spike: If an earnings report, product launch, or major macroeconomic report is approaching, uncertainty spikes. Traders rush to buy options for protection or speculation. This flood of buy orders drives up the market price (premium). As a result, the math back-solves a higher IV. [1] (https://www.schwab.com/learn/story/what-is-implied-volatility-basics-examples), [2] (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LCvjgUrx2HY&vl=en&t=390), [3] (https://www.cmcmarkets.com/en-gb/options-trading/what-is-implied-volatility), [4] (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=aYpLyeWu7Ac&t=572)
• The Volatility Crush: As soon as the news breaks, uncertainty is resolved. Demand for options instantly drops, causing premiums to deflate. The model registers lower options prices, and the back-solved IV plummets. [1] (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NEakTsocOEU), [2] (https://www.cmcmarkets.com/en-gb/options-trading/what-is-implied-volatility), [3] (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LCvjgUrx2HY&vl=en&t=390), [4] (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=aYpLyeWu7Ac&t=572)

import yfinance as yf
import pandas as pd
import numpy as np

def analyze_swing_sell_framework(ticker_symbol):
    """
    Evaluates a stock against the short-option swing trading framework.
    Uses 30-day Historical Volatility Rank as a reliable, free proxy for IV Rank.
    """
    print(f"=== Analyzing {ticker_symbol} for Short Option Swing Trades ===")
    
    # 1. Fetch 1 year of historical market data
    ticker = yf.Ticker(ticker_symbol)
    df = ticker.history(period="1y")
    
    if df.empty:
        print(f"Error: No data found for ticker '{ticker_symbol}'")
        return
        
    current_price = df['Close'].iloc[-1]
    
    # 2. Trend Filtering (Exponential Moving Averages)
    df['EMA_50'] = df['Close'].ewm(span=50, adjust=False).mean()
    df['EMA_200'] = df['Close'].ewm(span=200, adjust=False).mean()
    
    ema_50 = df['EMA_50'].iloc[-1]
    ema_200 = df['EMA_200'].iloc[-1]
    
    # Determine the directional bias
    if current_price > ema_50 and ema_50 > ema_200:
        trend_bias = "BULLISH"
        recommended_strategy = "Sell Cash-Secured Puts or Bull Put Credit Spreads"
    elif current_price < ema_50 and ema_50 < ema_200:
        trend_bias = "BEARISH"
        recommended_strategy = "Sell Covered Calls or Bear Call Credit Spreads"
    else:
        trend_bias = "NEUTRAL / CHOPPY"
        recommended_strategy = "Sell Iron Condors (Neutral / Range-bound)"

    # 3. Volatility Rank Calculation
    # Free APIs like yfinance don't output historical Implied Volatility. 
    # This block computes 30-day Historical Volatility (HV) and ranks it over the year.
    df['Returns'] = df['Close'].pct_change()
    df['HV_30'] = df['Returns'].rolling(window=21).std() * np.sqrt(252) * 100
    
    current_hv = df['HV_30'].iloc[-1]
    min_hv = df['HV_30'].min()
    max_hv = df['HV_30'].max()
    
    # Back-solve Volatility Rank (Scale of 0 to 100)
    if max_hv != min_hv:
        vol_rank = ((current_hv - min_hv) / (max_hv - min_hv)) * 100
    else:
        vol_rank = 0
        
    # Rule Checklist
    vol_filter_passed = vol_rank >= 50.0

    # 4. Generate Console Output Report
    print(f"Current Stock Price: ${current_price:.2f}")
    print(f"50 EMA: ${ema_50:.2f} | 200 EMA: ${ema_200:.2f} -> Trend Bias: {trend_bias}")
    print(f"Estimated Volatility Rank: {vol_rank:.1f}% -> {'HIGH (Overpriced Premium)' if vol_filter_passed else 'LOW (Underpriced Premium)'}")
    print("-" * 60)
    
    # 5. Output Signal Execution Rules
    if vol_filter_passed:
        print(f"🟢 SIGNAL: EXECUTE SHORT OPTION SWING")
        print(f"👉 Recommended Play: {recommended_strategy}")
        print(f"🎯 Execution Checklist (30-45 Days to Expiration):")
        if trend_bias == "BULLISH":
            print("   - Target short Put strikes at the 0.15 to 0.25 Delta level.")
        elif trend_bias == "BEARISH":
            print("   - Target short Call strikes at the 0.15 to 0.25 Delta level.")
        else:
            print("   - Target an Iron Condor: Sell both a Call and Put leg at 0.15 Delta.")
    else:
        print("🔴 SIGNAL: NO TRADE (Avoid Selling Premium)")
        print("👉 Rationale: Volatility is compressed. Premium values are too cheap to justify the tail risk.")

When swing trading short options, you do not wait for the contract to expire to realize your gains. Instead, you use specific indicators to buy back the option early (known as "closing to buy") to secure your profit and eliminate tail risk.

1. The Core Signal: The 50% Profit Target

The most reliable signal to buy back a sold option is when it has retained only 50% of its initial value.
• Why it works: Short options premium decay is non-linear. The risk/reward profile gets exponentially worse the longer you stay in a winning trade. Trying to squeeze out the remaining 50% of the premium takes just as long as the first 50%, but exposes you to 100% of a sudden market reversal.
• The Math: If you sold a Bull Put Spread for a $2.00 credit, your automated limit order should be set to buy it back as soon as the market price drops to $1.00.

2. The Time Signal: The 21 Days to Expiration (DTE) Rule

If your profit target has not been hit yet, you should buy back or roll the option when it hits 21 days left until expiration.
• Why it works: Beneath 21 DTE, "Gamma risk" accelerates. Gamma measures how fast your option's Delta changes. If the underlying stock makes a sharp move against you close to expiration, the option price will explode upward far faster than it would have at 45 DTE, quickly turning a winning trade into a maximum loss.

3. The Technical Analysis Exit Signal: Support/Resistance Touch

When swing trading, you want to buy back your short option if the underlying stock hits a major technical target on the price chart:
• For Cash-Secured Puts / Bull Put Spreads: Buy back the option when the stock price hits a major overhead resistance level or registers an RSI reading over 70 (overbought). The stock is likely to pull back from here, which will temporarily hurt your short put value.
• For Covered Calls / Bear Call Spreads: Buy back the option when the stock price drops to a key support level or registers an RSI below 30 (oversold).

Summary Checklist: When to Buy Back

Use this priority matrix to manage your short option exits:

Condition Type	Action Trigger	Tactical Rationale
Ideal Winner	50% of Max Profit achieved	Safely captures the meat of the move; frees up capital.
Time Limit	Trade reaches 21 DTE	Eliminates dangerous late-stage Gamma spikes.
Stop Loss	Option value hits 200% of initial credit	Defends capital; if you collected $1.00, cut losses if it hits $2.00.
Technical Pivot	Stock hits major Support/Resistance	Front-runs a potential short-term counter-trend reversal.

import yfinance as yf
import pandas as pd
import numpy as np
from datetime import datetime

def monitor_active_short_option(ticker_symbol, entry_date_str, entry_credit, dte_at_entry):
    """
    Monitors an active short option trade using the 50% Profit Take 
    and 21-Day Expiration exit rules.
    
    Parameters:
    - ticker_symbol (str): The stock ticker (e.g., 'AAPL')
    - entry_date_str (str): Date you sold the option ('YYYY-MM-DD')
    - entry_credit (float): The premium per contract you collected at entry (e.g., 2.00)
    - dte_at_entry (int): Days to expiration the contract had when you opened it
    """
    print(f"=== Monitoring Active Short Position: {ticker_symbol} ===")
    
    # 1. Calculate Days Elapsed and Remaining DTE
    entry_date = datetime.strptime(entry_date_str, "%Y-%m-%d").date()
    current_date = datetime.now().date()
    days_elapsed = (current_date - entry_date).days
    
    remaining_dte = dte_at_entry - days_elapsed
    
    # 2. Fetch Historical Data from Entry Date to Present
    ticker = yf.Ticker(ticker_symbol)
    df = ticker.history(start=entry_date_str)
    
    if df.empty:
        print(f"Error: Could not retrieve data for {ticker_symbol}.")
        return

    initial_stock_price = df['Close'].iloc[0]
    current_stock_price = df['Close'].iloc[-1]
    
    # 3. Simulate Current Option Value (Proxy Model)
    # Because free APIs do not provide live, historical options chain tracking data,
    # we approximate current option price movement using delta-based equity tracking
    # blended with a standard linear Theta decay over time.
    estimated_delta = 0.25  # Standard framework target entry delta
    price_change = current_stock_price - initial_stock_price
    
    # Intrinsic/Moneyness impact proxy
    underlying_impact = price_change * estimated_delta
    
    # Time decay component (Theta proxy tracking premium drop over time)
    time_decay_factor = remaining_dte / dte_at_entry if dte_at_entry > 0 else 0
    
    # Calculate estimated remaining market premium of your sold contract
    # For a put: stock going up reduces contract value (profitable for seller)
    estimated_current_premium = (entry_credit * time_decay_factor) - underlying_impact
    estimated_current_premium = max(0.01, estimated_current_premium) # Cannot drop below $0.01
    
    # Calculate current profit percentage achieved
    current_profit_pct = ((entry_credit - estimated_current_premium) / entry_credit) * 100
    profit_target_value = entry_credit * 0.50
    stop_loss_value = entry_credit * 2.00

    # 4. Print Active Trade Metrics Dashboard
    print(f"Entry Date: {entry_date_str} ({days_elapsed} days ago)")
    print(f"Stock Price Evolution: ${initial_stock_price:.2f} ➔ ${current_stock_price:.2f}")
    print(f"Remaining Time: {remaining_dte} DTE (Started at {dte_at_entry} DTE)")
    print(f"Collected Premium: ${entry_credit:.2f} | Target Buy-Back: ${profit_target_value:.2f}")
    print(f"Est. Current Premium: ${estimated_current_premium:.2f} ({current_profit_pct:.1f}% of max profit captured)")
    print("-" * 60)

    # 5. Rule Verification & Signal Routing
    if current_profit_pct >= 50.0:
        print("🟢 SIGNAL: BUY TO CLOSE (Take Profit Target Reached)")
        print(f"👉 Target Achieved: You captured {current_profit_pct:.1f}% of the premium.")
        print(f"👉 Execution: Route a limit order to buy back the contract at market price (${estimated_current_premium:.2f}) to lock in gains.")
        
    elif remaining_dte <= 21:
        print("🟡 SIGNAL: BUY TO CLOSE / ROLL POSITION (21-DTE Time Limit Rule reached)")
        print(f"👉 Rationale: Contract has hit {remaining_dte} days to expiration. Gamma risk is accelerating.")
        print("👉 Execution: Close the trade now to preserve remaining capital, or roll the strategy out to a new 45 DTE cycle.")
        
    elif estimated_current_premium >= stop_loss_value:
        print("🔴 SIGNAL: EMERGENCY EXIT (200% Capital Risk Stop Loss)")
        print(f"👉 Rationale: The contract premium has doubled against you (${estimated_current_premium:.2f}). Defend your remaining net worth.")
        
    else:
        print("⚪ SIGNAL: HOLD POSITION (No Action Required)")
        print("👉 Status: The trade is within safe parameters. Let Theta decay continue running down the premium clock.")

import yfinance as yf
import pandas as pd
import numpy as np
from datetime import datetime

def compute_rsi(series, period=14):
    """Calculates the Relative Strength Index (RSI) for a given price series."""
    delta = series.diff()
    gain = (delta.where(delta > 0, 0)).rolling(window=period).mean()
    loss = (-delta.where(delta < 0, 0)).rolling(window=period).mean()
    
    rs = gain / (loss + 1e-10)  # Avoid division by zero
    return 100 - (100 / (1 + rs))

def calculate_support_resistance(df, window=20):
    """
    Identifies recent key Support and Resistance boundaries 
    using local rolling extrema over a specified trading window.
    """
    recent_data = df.tail(60) # Review the last 2-3 months of action
    resistance = recent_data['High'].rolling(window=window, center=True).max().dropna().iloc[-1]
    support = recent_data['Low'].rolling(window=window, center=True).min().dropna().iloc[-1]
    return support, resistance

Yes, trading volume and Open Interest (OI) are absolutely critical, but they do not serve as buy or sell entry signals. Instead, they act as strict liquidity filters.
While indicators like IV Rank or Delta tell you which strategy to deploy and where to place your strike, Volume and Open Interest tell you if you can safely trade that stock at all.
If a stock lacks sufficient volume and open interest, you will be trapped in the trade by predatory market makers.

Why Open Interest and Volume Matter for Option Sellers


1. Preventing Bid-Ask Spread Bleed

When an option contract has low open interest, the gap between what buyers want to pay (Bid) and what sellers want to accept (Ask) widens significantly.
• The Trap: You might see an option with a theoretical value of $1.00. However, due to low liquidity, the market market bids $0.50 and asks $1.50.
• The Cost: If you try to buy it back early to hit your 50% profit target, you will be forced to pay the inflated Ask price, instantly wiping out your hard-earned premium gains.

2. Guaranteeing Slippage-Free Exits

As an option seller, your operational framework mandates buying back contracts at 50% profit or at 21 DTE. If a black swan event happens and you need to hit your 200% stop-loss, you need immediate execution. High Volume and OI guarantee that your market or limit order fills instantly without "slippage" (getting filled at a much worse price than expected).

The Liquidity Filter Checklist

Before checking your IV Rank or EMAs, apply these baseline liquidity rules to your chosen stock and specific options chain:

Metric	Minimum Target Requirement	Actionable Purpose
Stock Daily Volume	> 1 Million shares per day	Ensures the underlying stock itself is highly liquid and institutional.
Contract Open Interest	> 500 to 1,000 contracts	Measures open active positions on your specific strike; ensures market makers are active.
Contract Daily Volume	> 100 contracts traded today	Proves there is live, active retail and institutional interest at this exact moment.
The Penny Rule (Spread)	Spread ≤ $0.05 to $0.10	The maximum allowable gap between the Bid and Ask on your target strike.


Advanced Dynamic: Tracking "Unusual" Open Interest

For a swing trader selling options, a massive, sudden spike in volume or open interest on a specific out-of-the-money strike can act as a warning indicator.
If you are planning to sell a Cash-Secured Put, but you notice that open interest on near-term Put options suddenly doubled overnight, it means an institution is buying massive downside protection. This implies an imminent, sharp downward move is expected, signaling you to stand down and avoid selling that premium.

def compute_rsi(series, period=14):
    """Calculates the Relative Strength Index (RSI) for a given price series."""
    delta = series.diff()
    gain = (delta.where(delta > 0, 0)).rolling(window=period).mean()
    loss = (-delta.where(delta < 0, 0)).rolling(window=period).mean()
    rs = gain / (loss + 1e-10)
    return 100 - (100 / (1 + rs))

def calculate_support_resistance(df, window=20):
    """Identifies recent structural Support and Resistance zones using rolling extremes."""
    recent_data = df.tail(60)
    resistance = recent_data['High'].rolling(window=window, center=True).max().dropna().iloc[-1]
    support = recent_data['Low'].rolling(window=window, center=True).min().dropna().iloc[-1]
    return support, resistance

def fetch_live_contract_liquidity(ticker_symbol, target_strike, option_type):
    """
    Scrapes the live options chain via Yahoo Finance to pull exact 
    real-time Bid, Ask, Spread, and Open Interest data for the contract.
    """
    ticker = yf.Ticker(ticker_symbol)
    
    try:
        # Pull the closest standard near-term expiration dates array
        expirations = ticker.options
        if not expirations:
            return None
        
        # Pull the front-month chain (typically closest standard monthly option)
        chain = ticker.option_chain(expirations[0])
        chain_df = chain.puts if option_type.upper() == "PUT" else chain.calls
        
        # Find the row closest matching our targeted strike boundary
        matching_strike = chain_df.iloc[(chain_df['strike'] - target_strike).abs().argsort()[:1]]
        
        if matching_strike.empty:
            return None
            
        contract = matching_strike.iloc[0]
        bid = contract['bid']
        ask = contract['ask']
        open_interest = contract['openInterest']
        volume = contract['volume']
        
        # Avoid division-by-zero on illiquid unquoted bid-asks
        mid_price = (bid + ask) / 2
        spread = ask - bid
        spread_pct = (spread / mid_price) * 100 if mid_price > 0 else 999.0
        
        return {
            "bid": bid, "ask": ask, "spread_dollars": spread,
            "spread_pct": spread_pct, "open_interest": open_interest, "volume": volume
        }
    except Exception as e:
        # Fallback safeguard structure if options chain formatting blocks the query
        return None
